Why Kaomoji Are Cheap and Cool

rev 0.2 · notes · ~2 min read

Every image on a page costs something: bytes, a CDN request, an alt text, a designer's afternoon. A kaomoji costs a few characters. Here is the whole argument in one line:

(ノ◕ヮ◕)ノ*:・゚✧

Cheap, literally

A kaomoji is plain Unicode text. That means:

  • ~10–30 bytes versus 50–500 KB for an emoji sticker or reaction GIF — roughly 10,000× smaller.
  • Zero HTTP requests. It ships inside the HTML you were sending anyway.
  • No licensing, no asset pipeline, no image CDN. You type it, it exists.
  • Copy-paste durable. It survives plain-text email, RSS readers, terminals, and SMS — places where images die.
ᕕ( ᐛ )ᕗ

Cool, genuinely

Emoji are rendered by the platform — your 🙂 looks different on every device, and everyone reaches for the same fifty. Kaomoji are composed, not selected. A table flip carries more precise frustration than any yellow circle:

(╯°□°）╯︵ ┻━┻

…and putting the table back is a whole narrative arc in fourteen characters:

┬─┬ノ( º _ ºノ)

They read sideways-free — no head tilt required like :-) — because they borrowed the expressive eyes of Japanese typography. That is why (¬_¬) feels skeptical and (◕‿◕) feels sincere in a way Western smileys never quite managed.

Thousands exist. In practice a small set does almost all the work. Copy any row — they are plain text, so they paste anywhere.

How to use them without looking silly

  • Put it at the end. A kaomoji is punctuation, not a subject. It closes the thought and colours what came before it.
  • One per message. Two competing faces cancel each other out and read as noise.
  • Escape it in Markdown. _ means italics and \ escapes, so the shrug collapses into mush. Type ¯\\\_(ツ)\_/¯ to get ¯\_(ツ)_/¯, or wrap it in backticks.
  • Use utf8mb4, not utf8. MySQL's utf8 is three bytes and silently truncates. A face is many codepoints — a VARCHAR(20) can cut one mid-sequence and leave a replacement character.
  • Count characters, not glyphs. (づ｡◕‿‿◕｡)づ is one face but ten codepoints and twenty-six UTF-8 bytes. Anything with a length limit — SMS, a tweet, a commit subject — counts the codepoints.
  • Name a font stack. Most monospace and UI fonts carry no CJK glyphs, so each character falls back separately and the face pulls apart. Declare real CJK families first — this page does exactly that.
  • Mind screen readers. They announce these character by character. Keep them out of buttons, labels, and anything load-bearing; decorative use in prose is fine.
  • Save them as snippets. Text replacement on macOS, iOS, or your editor beats retyping combining characters by hand every time.
  • Skip them in formal writing. Incident reports, legal text, and customer apologies are worse with a face, not better.

The takeaway

If a picture is worth a thousand words, a kaomoji is a picture made of words — at one ten-thousandth the bandwidth. Cheap and cool are not a trade-off here. They are the same feature.
